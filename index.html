Matrices and Their Types

1. Matrix

Think of a matrix as a table or grid of information. Just like a spreadsheet (Excel/Google Sheets) has rows and columns filled with numbers, a matrix is the mathematical version of that grid.

Definition

A Matrix is a rectangular arrangement of numbers or elements in rows and columns, where each position is identified by its row and column.

1.1 General Form of an \(m \times n\) Matrix

To understand the basic terms clearly, let us take a general matrix with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & a_{13} & \cdots & a_{1n} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & a_{23} & \cdots & a_{2n} \\ a_{31} & a_{32} & a_{33} & \cdots & a_{3n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{m1} & a_{m2} & a_{m3} & \cdots & a_{mn} \end{bmatrix}_{m\times n} \]

Using this general matrix, we will now define each basic term one by one.

1.1.1 Element (or Entry)

Definition

An element (or entry) is a single number or symbol inside a matrix. It is identified by its position using two subscripts: the row number and the column number.

Notation: An element in the \(i\)-th row and \(j\)-th column is written as \(a_{ij}\).

Explanation: In the general matrix above, each number like \(a_{11}, a_{12}, a_{23}, a_{mn}\) is an element.

  • \(a_{11}\) means the element in the 1st row and 1st column.
  • \(a_{23}\) means the element in the 2nd row and 3rd column.
  • \(a_{mn}\) means the element in the \(m\)-th row and \(n\)-th column.
  • In general, \(a_{ij}\) means the element in the \(i\)-th row and \(j\)-th column.

Total number of elements: A matrix of order \(m \times n\) has exactly \(m \times n\) elements.

1.1.2 Row

Definition

A row is a horizontal arrangement of elements in a matrix. All the elements lying side by side in the same horizontal line form one row.

Explanation: In the general matrix, each horizontal line of elements is a row.

  • Row 1: \(a_{11}, a_{12}, a_{13}, \ldots, a_{1n}\)
  • Row 2: \(a_{21}, a_{22}, a_{23}, \ldots, a_{2n}\)
  • Row 3: \(a_{31}, a_{32}, a_{33}, \ldots, a_{3n}\)
  • Row \(m\): \(a_{m1}, a_{m2}, a_{m3}, \ldots, a_{mn}\)

Key point: The matrix has exactly \(m\) rows. Each row contains \(n\) elements.

1.1.3 Column

Definition

A column is a vertical arrangement of elements in a matrix. All the elements lying one below the other in the same vertical line form one column.

Explanation: In the general matrix, each vertical line of elements is a column.

  • Column 1: \(a_{11}, a_{21}, a_{31}, \ldots, a_{m1}\)
  • Column 2: \(a_{12}, a_{22}, a_{32}, \ldots, a_{m2}\)
  • Column 3: \(a_{13}, a_{23}, a_{33}, \ldots, a_{m3}\)
  • Column \(n\): \(a_{1n}, a_{2n}, a_{3n}, \ldots, a_{mn}\)

Key point: The matrix has exactly \(n\) columns. Each column contains \(m\) elements.

1.1.4 Order of a Matrix

Definition

The order (or size) of a matrix tells us how many rows and how many columns it has. It is written as:

\[ \text{Order} = \text{Number of rows} \times \text{Number of columns} \]

Explanation: For the general matrix \(A_{m \times n}\) above:

  • Number of rows = \(m\)
  • Number of columns = \(n\)
  • Therefore, the order of the matrix is \(m \times n\) (read as "\(m\) by \(n\)").
  • Total number of elements = \(m \times n\).

Important: The order is always written as rows first, then columns (R × C).

Principal Diagonal

Definition

In a square matrix, the principal diagonal (also called the main diagonal or leading diagonal) is the set of elements that run from the top-left corner to the bottom-right corner.

For a square matrix \(A = [a_{ij}]_{n \times n}\), the principal diagonal consists of elements:

\[ a_{11}, \ a_{22}, \ a_{33}, \ \ldots, \ a_{nn} \]

These are the elements where row number = column number \((i = j)\).

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{4} & 2 & 3 \\ 5 & \mathbf{7} & 6 \\ 1 & 8 & \mathbf{9} \end{bmatrix} \]

Here, principal diagonal elements are: \(\mathbf{4, \ 7, \ 9}\)

The other elements (2, 3, 5, 6, 1, 8) are called non-diagonal elements or off-diagonal elements.

Note

The term "principal diagonal" applies only to square matrices (matrices with equal number of rows and columns). Row, column, and rectangular matrices do not have a principal diagonal.

Now let us study each type of matrix.

Types of Matrices

Row Matrix

Definition

A matrix having only one row (but any number of columns) is called a Row Matrix.

General form:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1n} \end{bmatrix}_{1 \times n} \]

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 7 & 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Look at the matrix above. Count the rows — there is only one horizontal line of numbers. That is why it is called a row matrix.

Column Matrix

Definition

A matrix having only one column (but any number of rows) is called a Column Matrix.

General form:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} \\ a_{21} \\ \vdots \\ a_{m1} \end{bmatrix}_{m \times 1} \]

Example:

\[ B = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 9 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Look at the matrix above. Count the columns — there is only one vertical line of numbers. That is why it is called a column matrix.

Rectangular Matrix

Definition

A matrix in which the number of rows is not equal to the number of columns \((m \neq n)\) is called a Rectangular Matrix.

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Look at the shape of this matrix. It has 2 rows and 3 columns. The number of rows (2) is different from the number of columns (3). This mismatch in count makes it rectangular — just like a rectangle has unequal length and breadth.

Another Example:

\[ B = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 8 \\ 9 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}_{3 \times 2} \]
  • Rows = 3, Columns = 2. Since \(3 \neq 2\), it is rectangular.
  • No principal diagonal exists because it is not square.

Square Matrix

Definition

A matrix in which the number of rows equals the number of columns \((m = n)\) is called a Square Matrix.

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 7 & 4 \\ 6 & 9 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Count the rows and columns of this matrix. There are 3 rows and 3 columns — both counts are equal. This equal shape (like a square) is why it is called a square matrix.

This is the most important type because only square matrices can have a principal diagonal. All the special matrices that follow (diagonal, scalar, identity, triangular, symmetric, skew-symmetric) must be square matrices first.

Zero / Null Matrix

Definition

A matrix in which every element is zero is called a Zero Matrix or Null Matrix. It is denoted by \(O\).

Example:

\[ O = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Look at this matrix — every single number inside it is 0. There is nothing but zeros. That is why it is called a zero matrix.

Special property: If you add a zero matrix to any other matrix of the same order, the result is the same original matrix. That is, \(A + O = A\). This is why it acts like the number 0 in matrix arithmetic.

Square Zero Matrix Example:

\[ O_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}_{3 \times 3} \]

Diagonal Matrix

Definition

A square matrix in which all non-diagonal elements are zero is called a Diagonal Matrix.

In simple words: only the principal diagonal elements may be non-zero; everything outside the principal diagonal must be zero.

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Look at this matrix carefully. The elements 4, 7, 2 lie on the principal diagonal (top-left to bottom-right). Every other position — the positions not on the diagonal — contains 0.

Scalar Matrix

Definition

A diagonal matrix in which all principal diagonal elements are equal is called a Scalar Matrix.

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: This matrix is already a diagonal matrix (non-diagonal elements are all zero). Now look at the diagonal elements: they are 5, 5, 5 — all the same number.

Why the name "Scalar"? Because if you multiply any matrix by a scalar matrix, the effect is the same as multiplying that matrix by a single number (scalar). For example, multiplying by the scalar matrix above is the same as multiplying every element by 5.

Identity / Unit Matrix

Definition

A scalar matrix in which all principal diagonal elements are equal to 1 is called an Identity Matrix or Unit Matrix. It is denoted by \(I\).

Example:

\[ I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: This is a special case of the scalar matrix where the constant value on the diagonal is 1. All diagonal elements are 1, and all non-diagonal elements are 0.

  • Order = 3 × 3 → it is a square matrix, so it has a principal diagonal.
  • Principal diagonal elements: 1, 1, 1 (all equal to 1).
  • All non-diagonal elements are zero.
  • Key condition: \(a_{ij} = 0\) for \(i \neq j\), and \(a_{ii} = 1\) for every \(i\).
  • Special property: Multiplying any matrix by the identity matrix gives back the same matrix — \(AI = IA = A\). This is why it is called the "identity" matrix; it behaves just like the number 1 in ordinary multiplication.

Upper Triangular Matrix

Definition

A square matrix in which all elements below the principal diagonal are zero is called an Upper Triangular Matrix.

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 & 7 \\ 0 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Draw the principal diagonal from top-left (2) to bottom-right (6). Now look at everything below that diagonal. You will find only zeros there.

  • Order = 3 × 3 → it is a square matrix, so it has a principal diagonal.
  • Principal diagonal elements: 2, 3, 6.
  • Elements below the principal diagonal (where row number > column number) are all zero:
    • \(a_{21} = 0\) (row 2, column 1)
    • \(a_{31} = 0\) (row 3, column 1)
    • \(a_{32} = 0\) (row 3, column 2)
  • Elements on and above the principal diagonal (where row number ≤ column number) may be any number: 2, 5, 7, 3, 4, 6.
  • Key condition: \(a_{ij} = 0\) whenever \(i > j\).
  • The name "upper" comes from the fact that the non-zero part forms an upper triangle shape above the diagonal.

Lower Triangular Matrix

Definition

A square matrix in which all elements above the principal diagonal are zero is called a Lower Triangular Matrix.

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 5 & 7 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Draw the principal diagonal from top-left (3) to bottom-right (9). Now look at everything above that diagonal. You will find only zeros there.

  • Order = 3 × 3 → it is a square matrix, so it has a principal diagonal.
  • Principal diagonal elements: 3, 7, 9.
  • Elements above the principal diagonal (where row number < column number) are all zero:
    • \(a_{12} = 0\) (row 1, column 2)
    • \(a_{13} = 0\) (row 1, column 3)
    • \(a_{23} = 0\) (row 2, column 3)
  • Elements on and below the principal diagonal (where row number ≥ column number) may be any number: 3, 5, 7, 2, 4, 9.
  • Key condition: \(a_{ij} = 0\) whenever \(i < j\).
  • The name "lower" comes from the fact that the non-zero part forms a lower triangle shape below the diagonal.

Symmetric Matrix

Definition

A square matrix \(A\) is called Symmetric if \(A^T = A\), i.e., the element at row \(i\), column \(j\) equals the element at row \(j\), column \(i\).

\[ a_{ij} = a_{ji} \quad \text{for all } i, j \]

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 & 8 \\ 5 & 3 & 7 \\ 8 & 7 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: Imagine folding this matrix along its principal diagonal (2, 3, 4). If the numbers on one side match exactly with the numbers on the other side, the matrix is symmetric.

  • Order = 3 × 3 → it is a square matrix, so it has a principal diagonal.
  • Principal diagonal elements: 2, 3, 4.
  • Now compare the elements that are mirror images across the diagonal:
    • \(a_{12} = 5\) and \(a_{21} = 5\) → equal ✓
    • \(a_{13} = 8\) and \(a_{31} = 8\) → equal ✓
    • \(a_{23} = 7\) and \(a_{32} = 7\) → equal ✓
  • So the matrix looks the same when reflected across the principal diagonal.
  • Key condition: \(a_{ij} = a_{ji}\) for every pair \((i, j)\).
  • The transpose of \(A\) equals \(A\) itself: \(A^T = A\).
  • The diagonal elements can be any number — there is no restriction on them.

Skew-Symmetric Matrix

Definition

A square matrix \(A\) is called Skew-Symmetric if \(A^T = -A\), i.e., the element at row \(i\), column \(j\) is the negative of the element at row \(j\), column \(i\).

\[ a_{ij} = -a_{ji} \quad \text{for all } i, j \]
Important Property

All principal diagonal elements must be zero.

Reason: For any diagonal element, \(a_{ii} = -a_{ii}\), which gives \(2a_{ii} = 0\), so \(a_{ii} = 0\).

Example:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 & -5 \\ -3 & 0 & 7 \\ 5 & -7 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Explanation: This is like a symmetric matrix but with a twist — the mirror-image elements are negatives of each other instead of being equal.

  • Order = 3 × 3 → it is a square matrix, so it has a principal diagonal.
  • Principal diagonal elements: 0, 0, 0 (all zero — this is mandatory).
  • Now compare the mirror-image elements:
    • \(a_{12} = 3\) and \(a_{21} = -3\) → \(a_{12} = -a_{21}\) ✓
    • \(a_{13} = -5\) and \(a_{31} = 5\) → \(a_{13} = -a_{31}\) ✓
    • \(a_{23} = 7\) and \(a_{32} = -7\) → \(a_{23} = -a_{32}\) ✓
  • So every element is the negative of its mirror image across the principal diagonal.
  • Key condition: \(a_{ij} = -a_{ji}\) for every pair \((i, j)\).
  • The transpose of \(A\) equals \(-A\): \(A^T = -A\).
  • Since every diagonal element must satisfy \(a_{ii} = -a_{ii}\), all diagonal elements are forced to be zero.

Matrix Operations and Properties

A matrix is not only a rectangular arrangement of numbers but also an important mathematical object on which various operations can be performed. Matrix operations provide systematic methods for combining, transforming, and manipulating matrices. In this chapter, we study the fundamental operations on matrices and their important properties. The main operations include matrix addition, subtraction, scalar multiplication, matrix multiplication, and other related operations. Understanding these operations and their properties forms the foundation for solving systems of linear equations and many applications in mathematics, science, and engineering.

Matrix Operations

Matrix operations are mathematical procedures used to combine or transform matrices to obtain new matrices. These operations are performed according to specific rules based on the order and elements of the matrices involved. In this section, we discuss the basic operations on matrices, beginning with addition and subtraction, followed by scalar multiplication and matrix multiplication. We will also examine the important properties associated with these operations.

Matrix Addition

Definition

Matrix addition is the operation of adding two matrices of the same order by adding their corresponding elements. If \(A = [a_{ij}]\) and \(B = [b_{ij}]\) are both \(m \times n\), then \(A + B = [a_{ij} + b_{ij}]\).

Example: We check first that both matrices are \(2 \times 2\), so addition is allowed. We then add each element of \(A\) to the element sitting in the same position in \(B\).

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 7 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]

We add position by position:

\[ A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 2+3 & 5+4 \\ 7+6 & 1+8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 9 \\ 13 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

Matrix Subtraction

Definition

Matrix subtraction is the operation of subtracting one matrix from another of the same order by subtracting corresponding elements. If \(A = [a_{ij}]\) and \(B = [b_{ij}]\) are both \(m \times n\), then \(A - B = [a_{ij} - b_{ij}]\).

Example: We check both are \(2 \times 2\), so subtraction is valid. We take each element of \(A\) and subtract the element in the same position in \(B\).

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 6 \\ 4 & 10 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \]

We subtract position by position:

\[ A - B = \begin{bmatrix} 9-3 & 6-2 \\ 4-1 & 10-7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Scalar Multiplication

Definition

Scalar multiplication is the operation of multiplying every element of a matrix by a single number (called a scalar). If \(k\) is a scalar and \(A = [a_{ij}]\), then \(kA = [k \cdot a_{ij}]\).

Example: We multiply matrix \(A\) by the scalar \(3\). We write the scalar \(3\) outside the matrix, then multiply it into every element inside.

\[ 3A = 3 \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \times 2 & 3 \times (-1) \\ 3 \times 4 & 3 \times 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & -3 \\ 12 & 15 \end{bmatrix} \]

Matrix Multiplication

Definition

Matrix multiplication combines two matrices \(A\) (order \(m \times n\)) and \(B\) (order \(n \times p\)) to give a matrix \(AB\) of order \(m \times p\). The element in row \(i\), column \(j\) of \(AB\) is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\). The number of columns of \(A\) must equal the number of rows of \(B\).

Example (2×2)

We multiply two \(2 \times 2\) matrices. The result will also be \(2 \times 2\). We write both matrices in general letter form first so the pattern is clear.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} e & f \\ g & h \end{bmatrix} \]

For each entry, we multiply a row of \(A\) with a column of \(B\) and add:

\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} ae+bg & af+bh \\ ce+dg & cf+dh \end{bmatrix} \]

Now we substitute actual numbers into the same pattern:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} (1)(5)+(2)(7) & (1)(6)+(2)(8) \\ (3)(5)+(4)(7) & (3)(6)+(4)(8) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5+14 & 6+16 \\ 15+28 & 18+32 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{bmatrix} \]

Example (3×3)

We multiply two \(3 \times 3\) matrices. The result will also be \(3 \times 3\). Again, we first write the general letter form to see the pattern.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} j & k & l \\ m & n & o \\ p & q & r \end{bmatrix} \]

For each entry, we multiply a row of \(A\) with a column of \(B\) and add:

\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} aj+bm+cp & ak+bn+cq & al+bo+cr \\ dj+em+fp & dk+en+fq & dl+eo+fr \\ gj+hm+ip & gk+hn+iq & gl+ho+ir \end{bmatrix} \]

Now we substitute actual numbers into the same pattern:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} (1)(1)+(2)(0)+(3)(0) & (1)(0)+(2)(1)+(3)(0) & (1)(0)+(2)(0)+(3)(1) \\ (4)(1)+(5)(0)+(6)(0) & (4)(0)+(5)(1)+(6)(0) & (4)(0)+(5)(0)+(6)(1) \\ (7)(1)+(8)(0)+(9)(0) & (7)(0)+(8)(1)+(9)(0) & (7)(0)+(8)(0)+(9)(1) \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = A \]

This shows that multiplying by the identity matrix leaves the matrix unchanged.

Transpose

Definition

The transpose of a matrix \(A\), written \(A^T\), is obtained by turning its rows into columns (or equivalently, columns into rows). If \(A = [a_{ij}]\) is \(m \times n\), then \(A^T = [a_{ji}]\) is \(n \times m\).

Example: We find the transpose of a \(2 \times 3\) matrix. Each row of \(A\) is written vertically as a column of \(A^T\).

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \]

Row 1 becomes column 1, row 2 becomes column 2:

\[ A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \]

Determinant

Definition

The determinant is a single number associated with a square matrix. It tells us whether the matrix is invertible (non-zero determinant) or singular (zero determinant). It is denoted by \(\det(A)\) or \(|A|\).

General form: For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\), the determinant is the difference between the products of the diagonals:

\[ \det(A) = ad - bc \]

For a \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{bmatrix}\), we expand along the first row using \(2 \times 2\) minors with alternating signs:

\[ \det(A) = a\begin{vmatrix} e & f \\ h & i \end{vmatrix} - b\begin{vmatrix} d & f \\ g & i \end{vmatrix} + c\begin{vmatrix} d & e \\ g & h \end{vmatrix} \]

Example (2×2): We multiply the main diagonal and subtract the anti-diagonal product.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 8 \\ 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \det(A) = (3)(6) - (8)(4) = 18 - 32 = -14 \]

Example (3×3): We expand along the first row with alternating signs \(+ - +\).

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \det(A) = 1 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 8 & 10 \end{vmatrix} - 2 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 7 & 10 \end{vmatrix} + 3 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 5 \\ 7 & 8 \end{vmatrix} \]

Evaluating each \(2 \times 2\) determinant:

\[ = 1(50-48) - 2(40-42) + 3(32-35) = 1(2) - 2(-2) + 3(-3) = 2 + 4 - 9 = -3 \]

Singular and Non-Singular Matrix

Definition (Singular Matrix)

A square matrix \(A\) is called singular if its determinant is zero, i.e., \(\det(A) = 0\). A singular matrix has no inverse.

Definition (Non-Singular Matrix)

A square matrix \(A\) is called non-singular if its determinant is not zero, i.e., \(\det(A) \neq 0\). A non-singular matrix has an inverse.

Example 1 (Singular):

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \det(A) = (2)(2) - (4)(1) = 4 - 4 = 0 \]

Since \(\det(A) = 0\), the matrix \(A\) is singular. It has no inverse.

Example 2 (Non-Singular):

\[ B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \det(B) = (3)(4) - (1)(2) = 12 - 2 = 10 \]

Since \(\det(B) = 10 \neq 0\), the matrix \(B\) is non-singular. It has an inverse.

Key Point

The determinant decides whether a matrix is invertible.

  • Zero determinant \(\rightarrow\) singular (no inverse).
  • Non-zero determinant \(\rightarrow\) non-singular (inverse exists).

Minors

Definition

The minor of an element \(a_{ij}\) in a square matrix is the determinant of the smaller matrix obtained by deleting row \(i\) and column \(j\). It is denoted \(M_{ij}\).

Example: We find all minors of a \(3 \times 3\) matrix.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 3 & 5 & 7 \\ 6 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

For \(M_{11}\), we delete row 1 and column 1, leaving \(\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 7 \\ 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{11} = (5)(9) - (7)(8) = 45 - 56 = -11 \]

For \(M_{12}\), we delete row 1 and column 2, leaving \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 7 \\ 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{12} = (3)(9) - (7)(6) = 27 - 42 = -15 \]

For \(M_{13}\), we delete row 1 and column 3, leaving \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 5 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{13} = (3)(8) - (5)(6) = 24 - 30 = -6 \]

For \(M_{21}\), deleting row 2 and column 1 leaves \(\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{21} = (4)(9) - (1)(8) = 36 - 8 = 28 \]

For \(M_{22}\), deleting row 2 and column 2 leaves \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{22} = (2)(9) - (1)(6) = 18 - 6 = 12 \]

For \(M_{23}\), deleting row 2 and column 3 leaves \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{23} = (2)(8) - (4)(6) = 16 - 24 = -8 \]

For \(M_{31}\), deleting row 3 and column 1 leaves \(\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 5 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{31} = (4)(7) - (1)(5) = 28 - 5 = 23 \]

For \(M_{32}\), deleting row 3 and column 2 leaves \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{32} = (2)(7) - (1)(3) = 14 - 3 = 11 \]

For \(M_{33}\), deleting row 3 and column 3 leaves \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\):

\[ M_{33} = (2)(5) - (4)(3) = 10 - 12 = -2 \]

The minor matrix is:

\[ M = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & -15 & -6 \\ 28 & 12 & -8 \\ 23 & 11 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Cofactors

Definition

The cofactor of an element \(a_{ij}\), written \(C_{ij}\), is its minor with a sign attached: \(C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}\). The sign alternates in a checkerboard pattern starting with \(+\) at the top-left.

Example: We take the same matrix as before and apply the sign pattern to its minors.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 3 & 5 & 7 \\ 6 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

From the previous calculation, the minor matrix is:

\[ M = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & -15 & -6 \\ 28 & 12 & -8 \\ 23 & 11 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

We apply the sign pattern \(\begin{bmatrix} + & - & + \\ - & + & - \\ + & - & + \end{bmatrix}\) to each minor:

\[ C = \begin{bmatrix} +(-11) & -(-15) & +(-6) \\ -(28) & +(12) & -(-8) \\ +(23) & -(11) & +(-2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & 15 & -6 \\ -28 & 12 & 8 \\ 23 & -11 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Adjoint (Adjugate)

Definition

The adjoint of a square matrix \(A\), written \(\text{adj}(A)\), is the transpose of its cofactor matrix. That is, \(\text{adj}(A) = C^T\).

Example: We take the same matrix as before and find its adjoint using the cofactor matrix we just calculated.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 3 & 5 & 7 \\ 6 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

From the previous calculation, the cofactor matrix is:

\[ C = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & 15 & -6 \\ -28 & 12 & 8 \\ 23 & -11 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

We swap rows and columns to get the adjoint — the first row of \(C\) becomes the first column of \(\text{adj}(A)\), and so on:

\[ \text{adj}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & -28 & 23 \\ 15 & 12 & -11 \\ -6 & 8 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Inverse

Definition

The inverse of a square matrix \(A\), written \(A^{-1}\), satisfies \(AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I\). It exists only if \(\det(A) \neq 0\), and is given by:

\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \cdot \text{adj}(A) \]

Example: We take the same matrix as before and find its inverse.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 3 & 5 & 7 \\ 6 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

We first compute the determinant. Expanding along the first row:

\[ \det(A) = 2\begin{vmatrix} 5 & 7 \\ 8 & 9 \end{vmatrix} - 4\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 7 \\ 6 & 9 \end{vmatrix} + 1\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 5 \\ 6 & 8 \end{vmatrix} \]
\[ = 2(45-56) - 4(27-42) + 1(24-30) = 2(-11) - 4(-15) + 1(-6) = -22 + 60 - 6 = 32 \]

Since \(32 \neq 0\), the inverse exists.

From the previous calculation, the adjoint is:

\[ \text{adj}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} -11 & -28 & 23 \\ 15 & 12 & -11 \\ -6 & 8 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

We divide every element by the determinant \(32\):

\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{32}\begin{bmatrix} -11 & -28 & 23 \\ 15 & 12 & -11 \\ -6 & 8 & -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\frac{11}{32} & -\frac{28}{32} & \frac{23}{32} \\ \frac{15}{32} & \frac{12}{32} & -\frac{11}{32} \\ -\frac{6}{32} & \frac{8}{32} & -\frac{2}{32} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.344 & -0.875 & 0.719 \\ 0.469 & 0.375 & -0.344 \\ -0.188 & 0.25 & -0.063 \end{bmatrix} \]

Elementary Row Operations

Definition

Elementary row operations are simple transformations performed on the rows of a matrix. There are three types: (1) swap two rows, (2) multiply a row by a non-zero scalar, (3) add a multiple of one row to another row. These operations do not change the solution set of a linear system.

Notation: We represent each operation by writing it beside the matrix using arrow notation:

OperationNotationMeaning
Swap rows\(R_i \leftrightarrow R_j\)Interchange row \(i\) and row \(j\)
Scale a row\(R_i \to kR_i\)Multiply row \(i\) by the scalar \(k\)
Add multiple of a row\(R_i \to R_i + kR_j\)Add \(k\) times row \(j\) to row \(i\)

Here \(R_i\) means "Row \(i\)". The arrow \(\to\) means "we replace the row on the left with the result on the right."

Example: We apply all three types to a matrix, writing the operation we are performing beside each step.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 1 — Swap row 1 and row 3: We write \(R_1 \leftrightarrow R_3\). This exchanges their positions:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 \leftrightarrow R_3} \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 8 & 9 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 2 — Multiply row 2 by \(\frac{1}{2}\): We write \(R_2 \to \frac{1}{2}R_2\). This scales row 2 down:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 8 & 9 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 \to \frac{1}{2}R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 8 & 9 \\ 2 & 2.5 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 3 — Add \(-7\) times row 3 to row 1: We write \(R_1 \to R_1 + (-7)R_3\) or simply \(R_1 \to R_1 - 7R_3\). This eliminates the leading \(7\) in row 1:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 8 & 9 \\ 2 & 2.5 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 \to R_1 - 7R_3} \begin{bmatrix} 7-7 & 8-14 & 9-21 \\ 2 & 2.5 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -6 & -12 \\ 2 & 2.5 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Each step clearly shows which row we are changing and how the operation is written. This notation is used throughout linear algebra when solving systems of equations.

Elementary Column Operations

Definition

Elementary column operations are the column equivalents of row operations: (1) swap two columns, (2) multiply a column by a non-zero scalar, (3) add a multiple of one column to another column. They are used mainly when working with column-based methods.

Notation: We represent each operation by writing it above the matrix using arrow notation:

OperationNotationMeaning
Swap columns\(C_i \leftrightarrow C_j\)Interchange column \(i\) and column \(j\)
Scale a column\(C_i \to kC_i\)Multiply column \(i\) by the scalar \(k\)
Add multiple of a column\(C_i \to C_i + kC_j\)Add \(k\) times column \(j\) to column \(i\)

Here \(C_i\) means "Column \(i\)".

Example: We apply column operations to a matrix.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 1 — Swap column 1 and column 3: We write \(C_1 \leftrightarrow C_3\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{C_1 \leftrightarrow C_3} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 5 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 2 — Multiply column 2 by \(3\): We write \(C_2 \to 3C_2\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 5 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{C_2 \to 3C_2} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 & 1 \\ 6 & 15 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 3 — Add column 1 to column 3: We write \(C_3 \to C_3 + C_1\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 & 1 \\ 6 & 15 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{C_3 \to C_3 + C_1} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 & 1+3 \\ 6 & 15 & 4+6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 & 4 \\ 6 & 15 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \]

Row Reduction

Definition

Row reduction (Gaussian elimination) is the process of using elementary row operations to transform a matrix into row echelon form (zeros below each leading entry) or reduced row echelon form (leading 1s with zeros above and below). It is used to solve linear systems and find ranks and inverses.

Example: We reduce a \(3 \times 3\) matrix to row echelon form, writing the operation beside each step.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 3 & 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]

We swap row 1 and row 2 so a leading \(1\) comes to the top. We write \(R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 3 & 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 3 & 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]

We eliminate the entries below the leading \(1\). We apply \(R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1\) and \(R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_1\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 3 & 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1} \xrightarrow{R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 2-2 & 4-6 & 6-10 \\ 3-3 & 7-9 & 8-15 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 0 & -2 & -4 \\ 0 & -2 & -7 \end{bmatrix} \]

We scale row 2 by \(-\frac{1}{2}\). We write \(R_2 \to -\frac{1}{2}R_2\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 0 & -2 & -4 \\ 0 & -2 & -7 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 \to -\frac{1}{2}R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 & -7 \end{bmatrix} \]

We eliminate the entry below the leading \(1\) in row 2. We apply \(R_3 \to R_3 + 2R_2\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 & -7 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 \to R_3 + 2R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2+2 & -7+4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix} \]

This is row echelon form — a staircase pattern with zeros below each leading entry.

Inverse of Matrix (Row Operations Method)

Definition

A square matrix \(A\) of order \(n\) is said to be invertible if there exists another square matrix \(B\) of the same order \(n\) such that:

\[ AB = BA = I_n \]

The matrix \(B\) is called the inverse of \(A\), denoted by \(A^{-1}\).

\[ \boxed{AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I} \]
Condition

\(A\) must be a square matrix, and the inverse exists only if \(A\) is non-singular.

Identity Matrix Used

Before starting, we must identify the identity matrix \(I\) of the same order as \(A\).

  • If \(A\) is of order \(2 \times 2\), we use: \[ I_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]
  • If \(A\) is of order \(3 \times 3\), we use: \[ I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

In general, for an \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\), the identity matrix \(I_n\) has 1's on the main diagonal and 0's everywhere else.

Method

We know that:

\[ A^{-1} \cdot A = I \]

where \(I\) is the identity matrix of the same order as \(A\).

We apply row operations on both sides simultaneously.

After each row operation, we write:

\[ (A^{-1})\,[\text{changed } A] = [\text{changed } I] \]

We continue until the left side becomes \(I\). Then:

\[ A^{-1} \cdot I = A^{-1} \]

So the final right side is \(A^{-1}\).

Allowed Row Operations
  1. Interchange two rows: \(R_i \leftrightarrow R_j\)
  2. Multiply a row by a nonzero scalar: \(R_i \to kR_i\)
  3. Add a multiple of one row to another: \(R_i \to R_i + kR_j\)

Example 1: Inverse of a \(2 \times 2\) Matrix

Problem: Find the inverse of

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Identity matrix used (order \(2 \times 2\)):

\[ I_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

We know that:

\[ A^{-1} \cdot A = I_2 \]

So,

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 1: \(R_1 \to \frac{1}{2}R_1\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 2: \(R_2 \to R_2 - 5R_1\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} & 0 \\ -\frac{5}{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 3: \(R_2 \to 2R_2\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} & 0 \\ -5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 4: \(R_1 \to R_1 - \frac{1}{2}R_2\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Left side is now \(I_2\). Therefore:

\[ A^{-1} \cdot I_2 = A^{-1} \] \[ A^{-1} \cdot I_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ \boxed{A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}} \]

Example 2: Inverse of a \(3 \times 3\) Matrix

Problem: Find the inverse of

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Identity matrix used (order \(3 \times 3\)):

\[ I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

We know that:

\[ A^{-1} \cdot A = I_3 \]

So,

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 1: \(R_3 \to R_3 - 5R_1\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & -4 & -15 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -5 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 2: \(R_3 \to R_3 + 4R_2\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 3: \(R_2 \to R_2 - 4R_3\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 4: \(R_1 \to R_1 - 3R_3\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 16 & -12 & -3 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Operation 5: \(R_1 \to R_1 - 2R_2\)

\[ (A^{-1}) \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Left side is now \(I_3\). Therefore:

\[ A^{-1} \cdot I_3 = A^{-1} \] \[ A^{-1} \cdot I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ \boxed{A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}} \]

Summary

MethodIdentity UsedStart WithEnd With
Row Operations\(I_n\) (same order as \(A\))\(A^{-1} \cdot A = I_n\)\(A^{-1} \cdot I_n = A^{-1}\)
Key Point

We never assume the value of \(A^{-1}\). We first mention the identity matrix \(I_n\) of the same order as \(A\), then keep applying row operations until the matrix next to \(A^{-1}\) becomes \(I_n\), and the other side automatically becomes \(A^{-1}\).

Matrix Powers

Definition

For a square matrix \(A\), the \(n\)-th power \(A^n\) is the product of \(A\) with itself \(n\) times: \(A^n = A \cdot A \cdots A\) (\(n\) times). By convention \(A^0 = I\), and \(A^1 = A\). Powers are only defined for square matrices.

Example: We compute \(A^2\) and \(A^3\) for:

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

For \(A^2\), we multiply \(A\) by itself:

\[ A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix} (1)(1)+(2)(0) & (1)(2)+(2)(1) \\ (0)(1)+(1)(0) & (0)(2)+(1)(1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

For \(A^3 = A^2 \cdot A\):

\[ A^3 =\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} (1)(1)+(4)(0) & (1)(2)+(4)(1) \\ (0)(1)+(1)(0) & (0)(2)+(1)(1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Notice the pattern: \(A^n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2n \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

Trace

Definition

The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its principal diagonal elements: \(\text{tr}(A) = a_{11} + a_{22} + \cdots + a_{nn}\).

Example: We compute the trace of a \(3 \times 3\) matrix.

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 8 & 4 \\ 7 & 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

We pick out the diagonal elements and add them:

\[ \text{tr}(A) = 5 + 8 + 9 = 22 \]

A useful property: \(\text{tr}(AB) = \text{tr}(BA)\) for any compatible square matrices.

Elementary Matrices

Definition

An elementary matrix is a matrix obtained by performing one single elementary row operation on an identity matrix. Multiplying a matrix \(A\) on the left by an elementary matrix performs that same row operation on \(A\). They are the building blocks of row reduction.

Example: We build an elementary matrix that swaps rows and apply it.

Start with the \(3 \times 3\) identity:

\[ I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

We perform one row operation — swap row 1 and row 3 — to get the elementary matrix:

\[ E = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

We multiply \(E\) on the left of \(A\):

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ EA = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 8 & 9 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

This confirms that the elementary matrix encoded exactly the row swap we wanted.

Properties of Matrices

Commutative Property

Definition

Matrix addition is commutative, meaning the order of addition does not matter:

\[ A + B = B + A \]

Explanation: Since we add corresponding elements (ordinary numbers), and number addition is commutative, matrix addition is also commutative.

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 1+5 & 2+6 \\ 3+7 & 4+8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 10 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ B + A = \begin{bmatrix} 5+1 & 6+2 \\ 7+3 & 8+4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 10 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; A + B = B + A \quad \checkmark \]

Associative Property

Definition

Matrix addition is associative, meaning the grouping of matrices does not matter:

\[ (A + B) + C = A + (B + C) \]

Explanation: Whether we add \(A\) and \(B\) first, then add \(C\), or add \(B\) and \(C\) first, then add \(A\), the result is the same because element-wise addition of numbers is associative.

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Left side: \((A + B) + C\)

\[ A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 10 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ (A + B) + C = \begin{bmatrix} 6+2 & 8+1 \\ 10+0 & 12+3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 9 \\ 10 & 15 \end{bmatrix} \]

Right side: \(A + (B + C)\)

\[ B + C = \begin{bmatrix} 5+2 & 6+1 \\ 7+0 & 8+3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 7 \\ 7 & 11 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A + (B + C) = \begin{bmatrix} 1+7 & 2+7 \\ 3+7 & 4+11 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 9 \\ 10 & 15 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; (A + B) + C = A + (B + C) \quad \checkmark \]

Additive Identity

Definition

The additive identity is the Zero Matrix \(O\), which when added to any matrix \(A\), gives back \(A\) itself:

\[ A + O = O + A = A \]

Explanation: The zero matrix has all elements equal to zero. Adding zero to each element of \(A\) leaves it unchanged. The zero matrix must be of the same order as \(A\).

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad O = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A + O = \begin{bmatrix} 1+0 & 2+0 \\ 3+0 & 4+0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = A \quad \checkmark \]
\[ O + A = \begin{bmatrix} 0+1 & 0+2 \\ 0+3 & 0+4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = A \quad \checkmark \]

Additive Inverse

Definition

The additive inverse of a matrix \(A\) is \(-A\) (negative of \(A\)), obtained by changing the sign of every element. When \(A\) is added to \(-A\), the result is the Zero Matrix:

\[ A + (-A) = (-A) + A = O \]

Explanation: Each element \(a_{ij}\) has an additive inverse \(-a_{ij}\) in ordinary arithmetic, so \(a_{ij} + (-a_{ij}) = 0\). Hence the sum of \(A\) and \(-A\) is the zero matrix.

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad -A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ -3 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A + (-A) = \begin{bmatrix} 1-1 & 2-2 \\ 3-3 & 4-4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = O \quad \checkmark \]
\[ (-A) + A = \begin{bmatrix} -1+1 & -2+2 \\ -3+3 & -4+4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = O \quad \checkmark \]

Subtraction of Matrices

Definition

Subtraction of matrices is defined as addition of the negative. Matrices must have the same order:

\[ A - B = A + (-B) \]

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]

\(A - B\):

\[ A - B = \begin{bmatrix} 1-5 & 2-6 \\ 3-7 & 4-8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & -4 \\ -4 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \]

\(B - A\):

\[ B - A = \begin{bmatrix} 5-1 & 6-2 \\ 7-3 & 8-4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]

Showing \(A - B \neq B - A\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} -4 & -4 \\ -4 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \neq \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; A - B \neq B - A \quad \checkmark \]

Explanation: Subtraction is not commutative because \(a_{ij} - b_{ij} \neq b_{ij} - a_{ij}\) in general (e.g., \(1 - 5 = -4\) but \(5 - 1 = 4\)). The only case where \(A - B = B - A\) is when \(A = B\), giving the zero matrix.

Other Properties:

  • Not Associative: \((A - B) - C \neq A - (B - C)\)
  • \(A - O = A\)
  • \(A - A = O\)

Scalar Multiplication (Properties)

Definition

Multiplying a matrix by a scalar \(k\) means multiplying every element by \(k\).

Example: Let \(k = 3\) and

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ kA = 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 9 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \]

Commutative Property (with scalar)

Definition

The order of scalar and matrix does not matter:

\[ kA = Ak \]

Explanation: Multiplying each element by \(k\) is the same whether \(k\) is written before or after the matrix.

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ 3A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 9 & 12 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A \cdot 3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 3 & 2 \cdot 3 \\ 3 \cdot 3 & 4 \cdot 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 9 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; 3A = A \cdot 3 \quad \checkmark \]

Associative Property (with scalar)

Definition

For scalars \(k\) and \(l\), the grouping of scalar multiplication does not matter:

\[ (kl)A = k(lA) \]

Explanation: Multiplying all elements by \(k\) first, then by \(l\), is the same as multiplying all elements by \((kl)\) at once.

Example: Let \(k = 2\), \(l = 3\), and

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ (kl)A = (2 \times 3)A = 6A = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 12 \\ 18 & 24 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ k(lA) = 2(3A) = 2\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 9 & 12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 12 \\ 18 & 24 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; (kl)A = k(lA) \quad \checkmark \]

Identity Property

Definition

Multiplying by scalar 1 gives back the same matrix:

\[ 1 \cdot A = A \]

Explanation: Each element multiplied by 1 remains unchanged.

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ 1 \cdot A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 2 \\ 1 \cdot 3 & 1 \cdot 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = A \quad \checkmark \]

Zero Property

Definition

Multiplying by scalar 0 gives the Zero Matrix:

\[ 0 \cdot A = O \]

Explanation: Each element multiplied by 0 becomes 0.

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ 0 \cdot A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = O \quad \checkmark \]

Distributive Property (over matrix addition)

Definition

Scalar multiplication distributes over matrix addition:

\[ k(A + B) = kA + kB \]

Example: Let \(k = 2\) and

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 10 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ k(A + B) = 2\begin{bmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 10 & 12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 16 \\ 20 & 24 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ kA + kB = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 12 \\ 14 & 16 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 16 \\ 20 & 24 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; k(A + B) = kA + kB \quad \checkmark \]

Distributive Property (over scalar addition)

Definition
\[ (k + l)A = kA + lA \]

Example: Let \(k = 2\), \(l = 3\), and

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ (k + l)A = 5A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 15 & 20 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ kA + lA = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 9 & 12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 15 & 20 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; (k + l)A = kA + lA \quad \checkmark \]

Commutative Is Not Valid Under Matrix Multiplication

Definition

If \(A\) is of order \(m \times n\) and \(B\) is of order \(n \times p\), then \(AB\) is of order \(m \times p\). Element \((i,j)\) of \(AB\) = sum of products of row \(i\) of \(A\) and column \(j\) of \(B\). The number of columns of \(A\) must equal the number of rows of \(B\).

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \]

\(AB\):

\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1(5)+2(7) & 1(6)+2(8) \\ 3(5)+4(7) & 3(6)+4(8) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{bmatrix} \]

\(BA\):

\[ BA = \begin{bmatrix} 5(1)+6(3) & 5(2)+6(4) \\ 7(1)+8(3) & 7(2)+8(4) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 23 & 34 \\ 31 & 46 \end{bmatrix} \]

Showing \(AB \neq BA\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{bmatrix} \neq \begin{bmatrix} 23 & 34 \\ 31 & 46 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; AB \neq BA \quad \checkmark \]

Explanation: Matrix multiplication is not commutative because the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\) is generally different from the dot product of row \(i\) of \(B\) with column \(j\) of \(A\).

Associative Property Under Matrix Multiplication

Definition
\[ (AB)C = A(BC) \]

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Left side: \((AB)C\)

\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ (AB)C = \begin{bmatrix} 19(2)+22(0) & 19(1)+22(3) \\ 43(2)+50(0) & 43(1)+50(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 38 & 85 \\ 86 & 193 \end{bmatrix} \]

Right side: \(A(BC)\)

\[ BC = \begin{bmatrix} 5(2)+6(0) & 5(1)+6(3) \\ 7(2)+8(0) & 7(1)+8(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 23 \\ 14 & 31 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A(BC) = \begin{bmatrix} 1(10)+2(14) & 1(23)+2(31) \\ 3(10)+4(14) & 3(23)+4(31) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 38 & 85 \\ 86 & 193 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; (AB)C = A(BC) \quad \checkmark \]

Distributive Property (Left)

Definition
\[ A(B + C) = AB + AC \]

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ B + C = \begin{bmatrix} 5+2 & 6+1 \\ 7+0 & 8+3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 7 \\ 7 & 11 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A(B + C) = \begin{bmatrix} 1(7)+2(7) & 1(7)+2(11) \\ 3(7)+4(7) & 3(7)+4(11) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 21 & 29 \\ 49 & 65 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ AB + AC = \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 6 & 15 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 21 & 29 \\ 49 & 65 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; A(B + C) = AB + AC \quad \checkmark \]

Distributive Property (Right)

Definition
\[ (A + B)C = AC + BC \]

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 10 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ (A + B)C = \begin{bmatrix} 6(2)+8(0) & 6(1)+8(3) \\ 10(2)+12(0) & 10(1)+12(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 30 \\ 20 & 46 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ AC + BC = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 6 & 15 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 23 \\ 14 & 31 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 30 \\ 20 & 46 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \therefore \; (A + B)C = AC + BC \quad \checkmark \]

Identity Property (Matrix Multiplication)

Definition
\[ AI = IA = A \]

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ AI = \begin{bmatrix} 1(1)+2(0) & 1(0)+2(1) \\ 3(1)+4(0) & 3(0)+4(1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = A \quad \checkmark \]
\[ IA = \begin{bmatrix} 1(1)+0(3) & 1(2)+0(4) \\ 0(1)+1(3) & 0(2)+1(4) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = A \quad \checkmark \]

Zero Property (Matrix Multiplication)

Definition
\[ AO = OA = O \]

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad O = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ AO = \begin{bmatrix} 1(0)+2(0) & 1(0)+2(0) \\ 3(0)+4(0) & 3(0)+4(0) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = O \quad \checkmark \]

Non-Commutative Property (Summary)

Definition
\[ AB \neq BA \quad \text{(in general)} \]

Explanation: As shown above, \(AB = \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{bmatrix}\) while \(BA = \begin{bmatrix} 23 & 34 \\ 31 & 46 \end{bmatrix}\). The order of multiplication changes the result.

Special Case: \(AB = BA\) only when:

  • \(A = B\), or
  • One is identity matrix, or
  • One is zero matrix, or
  • One is inverse of the other (\(AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I\))

Cancellation Law Does NOT Hold

Definition

If \(AB = AC\), it does NOT necessarily mean \(B = C\).

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad C = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad AC = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Here \(AB = AC\) but \(B \neq C\). \(\checkmark\)

Properties of Elementary Row Operations

Definition

Elementary row operations are simple transformations performed on the rows of a matrix. There are three types:

  1. Interchange two rows: \(R_i \leftrightarrow R_j\)
  2. Multiply a row by nonzero scalar: \(R_i \to kR_i\)
  3. Add a multiple of one row to another: \(R_i \to R_i + kR_j\)
Effects on Determinant
  • Swap \(\Rightarrow\) det changes sign
  • Multiply \(\Rightarrow\) det multiplied by \(k\)
  • Addition \(\Rightarrow\) det unchanged

Use: Finding inverse, rank, solving systems (Gauss elimination).

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A \xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - 3R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Properties of Elementary Column Operations

Definition

Same three operations applied to columns (\(C_i\)):

  1. \(C_i \leftrightarrow C_j\)
  2. \(C_i \to kC_i\)
  3. \(C_i \to C_i + kC_j\)
Properties
  • Same effect on determinant as row operations
  • Column operations correspond to right multiplication by elementary matrices
  • Used to find rank, inverse, normal form (PAQ form)

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ A \xrightarrow{C_2 \to C_2 - 2C_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Equality of Matrices

Definition

Two matrices \(A\) and \(B\) are said to be equal if they have the same order and their corresponding elements are equal.

If \(A = [a_{ij}]_{m \times n}\) and \(B = [b_{ij}]_{m \times n}\), then:

\[ A = B \iff a_{ij} = b_{ij} \quad \text{for all } i, j \]

In simple words: Same order + Same corresponding elements = Equal matrices.

General Form

If

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 2}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} b_{11} & b_{12} \\ b_{21} & b_{22} \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 2} \]

Then \(A = B\) means:

\[ a_{11} = b_{11}, \quad a_{12} = b_{12}, \quad a_{21} = b_{21}, \quad a_{22} = b_{22} \]

Key Conditions

  1. Same Order: Both matrices must have the same number of rows and columns.
  2. Element-wise Equality: Every element of \(A\) must match the corresponding element of \(B\).
If even one element differs, or if the orders are different, the matrices are not equal.

Example 1: Equal Matrices (Same Order, Same Elements)

Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Step 1: Check order.

  • Order of \(A = 2 \times 2\)
  • Order of \(B = 2 \times 2\) — Same order.

Step 2: Compare corresponding elements.

\[ a_{11} = 2 = b_{11} \] \[ a_{12} = 5 = b_{12} \] \[ a_{21} = -1 = b_{21} \] \[ a_{22} = 3 = b_{22} \]

Since both order and all elements match:

\[ \boxed{A = B} \]

Example 2: Not Equal (Same Order, Different Elements)

Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 7 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \]

Step 1: Check order.

  • Both are \(2 \times 2\) — Same order.

Step 2: Compare corresponding elements.

\[ a_{11} = 1 = b_{11} \] \[ a_{12} = 4 = b_{12} \] \[ a_{21} = 7 = b_{21} \] \[ a_{22} = 2 \neq 5 = b_{22} \]

Since \(a_{22} \neq b_{22}\):

\[ \boxed{A \neq B} \]

Example 3: Not Equal (Different Order)

Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 2}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}_{2 \times 3} \]

Step 1: Check order.

  • Order of \(A = 2 \times 2\)
  • Order of \(B = 2 \times 3\)

Since orders are different:

\[ \boxed{A \neq B} \]
Even if some elements look the same, different orders means the matrices are not equal.

Key Takeaway

  • Equality of matrices means:
    1. Same order
    2. Same corresponding elements
  • If \(A = B\), we can equate corresponding elements to form equations and solve for unknowns.
\[ \boxed{A = B \iff \text{(i) same order, (ii) } a_{ij} = b_{ij} \ \forall \ i, j} \]

Theorems on Matrices and Determinants

Theorems on Matrices

Theorem 1: Symmetric and Skew Symmetric from Any Square Matrix

Statement

For any square matrix \(A\) with real number entries, \(A + A'\) is a symmetric matrix and \(A - A'\) is a skew symmetric matrix.

Proof:

Let \(B = A + A'\), then

\[ \begin{align*} B' &= (A + A')' \\ &= A' + (A')' \quad \text{(as } (A + B)' = A' + B' \text{)} \\ &= A' + A \quad \text{(as } (A')' = A \text{)} \\ &= A + A' \quad \text{(as } A + B = B + A \text{)} \\ &= B \end{align*} \]

Therefore \(B = A + A'\) is a symmetric matrix.

Now let

\[ C = A - A' \]
\[ \begin{align*} C' &= (A - A')' = A' - (A')' \\ &= A' - A \\ &= -(A - A') = -C \end{align*} \]

Therefore \(C = A - A'\) is a skew symmetric matrix.

\[ \boxed{\therefore A + A' \text{ is symmetric and } A - A' \text{ is skew symmetric.}} \quad \blacksquare \]

Theorem 2: Decomposition into Symmetric and Skew Symmetric

Statement

Any square matrix can be expressed as the sum of a symmetric and a skew symmetric matrix.

Proof:

Let \(A\) be a square matrix, then we can write

\[ A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A') + \frac{1}{2}(A - A') \]

From Theorem 1, we know that \((A + A')\) is a symmetric matrix and \((A - A')\) is a skew symmetric matrix. Since for any matrix \(A\), \((kA)' = kA'\), it follows that \(\frac{1}{2}(A + A')\) is symmetric matrix and \(\frac{1}{2}(A - A')\) is skew symmetric matrix.

Thus, any square matrix can be expressed as the sum of a symmetric and a skew symmetric matrix.

\[ \boxed{\therefore A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A') + \frac{1}{2}(A - A')} \quad \blacksquare \]

Theorem 3: Uniqueness of Inverse

Statement

Inverse of a square matrix, if it exists, is unique.

Proof:

Let \(A = [a_{ij}]\) be a square matrix of order \(m\). If possible, let \(B\) and \(C\) be two inverses of \(A\). We shall show that \(B = C\).

Since \(B\) is the inverse of \(A\)

\[ AB = BA = I \quad \text{... (1)} \]

Since \(C\) is also the inverse of \(A\)

\[ AC = CA = I \quad \text{... (2)} \]

Thus

\[ B = BI = B(AC) = (BA)C = IC = C \]
\[ \boxed{\therefore \text{Inverse of a square matrix, if it exists, is unique.}} \quad \blacksquare \]

Theorem 4: Inverse of Product

Statement

If \(A\) and \(B\) are invertible matrices of the same order, then \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\).

Proof:

From the definition of inverse of a matrix, we have

\[ (AB)(AB)^{-1} = I \]
\[ A^{-1}(AB)(AB)^{-1} = A^{-1}I \quad \text{(Pre multiplying both sides by } A^{-1} \text{)} \]
\[ (A^{-1}A)B(AB)^{-1} = A^{-1}I \quad \text{(Since } A^{-1}I = A^{-1} \text{)} \]
\[ IB(AB)^{-1} = A^{-1} \]
\[ B(AB)^{-1} = A^{-1} \]
\[ B^{-1}B(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} \]
\[ I(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} \]

Hence

\[ \boxed{\therefore (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}} \quad \blacksquare \]

Theorems on Determinants

Theorem 1: Adjoint Property

Statement

If \(A\) is any square matrix of order \(n\), then

\[ A(\operatorname{adj} A) = (\operatorname{adj} A)A = |A| I \]

where \(I\) is the identity matrix of order \(n\).

Proof:

Let \(A = [a_{ij}]_{n \times n}\) and let \(A_{ij}\) be the cofactor of \(a_{ij}\).

By definition:

\[ \operatorname{adj} A = [A_{ij}]^T = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{21} & \cdots & A_{n1} \\ A_{12} & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{n2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ A_{1n} & A_{2n} & \cdots & A_{nn} \end{bmatrix} \]

Now consider the product \(A(\operatorname{adj} A)\). The \((i,j)\)-th element of this product is:

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} A_{jk} \]

Case 1: \(i = j\)

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} A_{ik} = a_{i1}A_{i1} + a_{i2}A_{i2} + \cdots + a_{in}A_{in} = |A| \]

(Sum of products of elements of a row with their corresponding cofactors equals the determinant.)

Case 2: \(i \neq j\)

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} A_{jk} = a_{i1}A_{j1} + a_{i2}A_{j2} + \cdots + a_{in}A_{jn} = 0 \]

(Sum of products of elements of one row with cofactors of another row is zero.)

Therefore:

\[ A(\operatorname{adj} A) = \begin{bmatrix} |A| & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & |A| & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & |A| \end{bmatrix} = |A| \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix} = |A| I \]

Similarly, \((\operatorname{adj} A)A = |A| I\).

\[ \boxed{\therefore A(\operatorname{adj} A) = (\operatorname{adj} A)A = |A| I} \quad \blacksquare \]

Theorem 2: Nonsingular Product

Statement

If \(A\) and \(B\) are nonsingular matrices of the same order, then \(AB\) and \(BA\) are also nonsingular matrices of the same order.

Proof:

Given: \(A\) and \(B\) are nonsingular matrices of order \(n\).

\[ \therefore |A| \neq 0 \quad \text{and} \quad |B| \neq 0 \]

Consider the product \(AB\). Since \(A\) and \(B\) are square matrices of order \(n\), \(AB\) is also a square matrix of order \(n\).

Using the property \(|AB| = |A| |B|\):

\[ |AB| = |A| \cdot |B| \]

Since \(|A| \neq 0\) and \(|B| \neq 0\):

\[ |AB| \neq 0 \]

Therefore, \(AB\) is nonsingular.

Similarly:

\[ |BA| = |B| \cdot |A| \neq 0 \]

Therefore, \(BA\) is also nonsingular.

\[ \boxed{\therefore \text{\(AB\) and \(BA\) are nonsingular matrices of the same order.}} \quad \blacksquare \]

Theorem 3: Product Determinant

Statement

The determinant of the product of matrices is equal to the product of their respective determinants. That is, \(|AB| = |A| |B|\), where \(A\) and \(B\) are square matrices of the same order.

Proof (for \(2 \times 2\) matrices):

Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} e & f \\ g & h \end{bmatrix} \]

Then:

\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e & f \\ g & h \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ae + bg & af + bh \\ ce + dg & cf + dh \end{bmatrix} \]

Now:

\[ |AB| = (ae + bg)(cf + dh) - (af + bh)(ce + dg) \]

Expanding:

\[ = aecf + aedh + bgcf + bgdh - afce - afdg - bhce - bhdg \]

Rearranging:

\[ = aecf - afce + aedh - afdg + bgcf - bhce + bgdh - bhdg \] \[ = ae(cf) - af(ce) + ad(eh) - ad(fg) + bc(gf) - bc(he) + bg(dh) - bh(dg) \]

Grouping terms:

\[ = (ad - bc)(eh - fg) \] \[ = |A| \cdot |B| \]
\[ \boxed{\therefore |AB| = |A| |B|} \quad \blacksquare \]

Note: The same proof extends to \(n \times n\) matrices.

Theorem 4: Invertibility Condition

Statement

A square matrix \(A\) is invertible if and only if \(A\) is a nonsingular matrix.

Proof:

We need to prove both directions.

Part 1: If \(A\) is invertible, then \(A\) is nonsingular.

Let \(A\) be an invertible matrix of order \(n\). Then there exists a square matrix \(B\) of order \(n\) such that:

\[ AB = BA = I \]

Taking determinants on both sides of \(AB = I\):

\[ |AB| = |I| \]

Using \(|AB| = |A| |B|\) and \(|I| = 1\):

\[ |A| \cdot |B| = 1 \]

Since the product of \(|A|\) and \(|B|\) equals 1, neither can be zero:

\[ |A| \neq 0 \]

Therefore, \(A\) is nonsingular.

Part 2: If \(A\) is nonsingular, then \(A\) is invertible.

Let \(A\) be a nonsingular matrix. Then:

\[ |A| \neq 0 \]

By Theorem 1:

\[ A(\operatorname{adj} A) = (\operatorname{adj} A)A = |A| I \]

Dividing both sides by \(|A|\) (which is nonzero):

\[ A\left(\frac{1}{|A|} \operatorname{adj} A\right) = \left(\frac{1}{|A|} \operatorname{adj} A\right)A = I \]

Let \(B = \frac{1}{|A|} \operatorname{adj} A\). Then:

\[ AB = BA = I \]

This means \(B\) is the inverse of \(A\). Therefore, \(A\) is invertible, and:

\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{|A|} \operatorname{adj} A \]
\[ \boxed{\therefore \text{\(A\) is invertible} \iff \text{\(A\) is nonsingular.}} \quad \blacksquare \]

Theorem 5: Adjoint Determinant

Statement

For a square matrix \(A\) of order \(n\):

\[ |\operatorname{adj} A| = |A|^{n-1} \]

Proof:

From Theorem 1:

\[ A(\operatorname{adj} A) = |A| I \]

Taking determinants on both sides:

\[ |A(\operatorname{adj} A)| = ||A| I| \]

Using \(|AB| = |A||B|\) on the left side:

\[ |A| \cdot |\operatorname{adj} A| = ||A| I| \]

Now, \(|A|\) is a scalar, and for a scalar \(k\) and \(n \times n\) identity matrix \(I\):

\[ |kI| = k^n |I| = k^n \]

So:

\[ |A| \cdot |\operatorname{adj} A| = |A|^n \]

Since \(A\) is nonsingular (\(|A| \neq 0\)), divide both sides by \(|A|\):

\[ |\operatorname{adj} A| = |A|^{n-1} \]
\[ \boxed{\therefore |\operatorname{adj} A| = |A|^{n-1}} \quad \blacksquare \]

Example (\(n = 3\)):

\[ |\operatorname{adj} A| = |A|^{3-1} = |A|^2 \]

Theorem 6: Inverse Determinant

Statement

If \(A\) is invertible, then:

\[ |A^{-1}| = \frac{1}{|A|} \]

Proof:

Since \(A\) is invertible:

\[ AA^{-1} = I \]

Taking determinants:

\[ |AA^{-1}| = |I| \] \[ |A| \cdot |A^{-1}| = 1 \]

Since \(|A| \neq 0\):

\[ |A^{-1}| = \frac{1}{|A|} \]
\[ \boxed{\therefore |A^{-1}| = \frac{1}{|A|}} \quad \blacksquare \]

Theorem 7: Inverse of Product

Statement

If \(A\) and \(B\) are invertible matrices of the same order, then:

\[ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1} \]

Proof:

To prove \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\), we show that:

\[ (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = I \quad \text{and} \quad (B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = I \]

First:

\[ (AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = A(I)A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I \]

Second:

\[ (B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}(I)B = B^{-1}B = I \]

Since both products equal \(I\), by definition of inverse:

\[ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} \]
\[ \boxed{\therefore (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}} \quad \blacksquare \]

Properties of Determinants

Properties

For square matrix \(A\) of order \(n\):

  • \(\det(A^T) = \det(A)\)
  • \(\det(AB) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)\)
  • \(\det(kA) = k^n \det(A)\)
  • \(\det(A^{-1}) = \dfrac{1}{\det(A)}\)
  • If any row/column is zero \(\Rightarrow \det = 0\)
  • If two rows/columns are identical \(\Rightarrow \det = 0\)
  • Swapping two rows changes sign of determinant
  • Adding a multiple of one row to another does not change determinant
  • \(\det(I) = 1\), \(\det(O) = 0\)

Example: Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ \det(A) = (1)(4) - (2)(3) = -2 \]

Properties of Adjoint

Definition

\(\text{adj}(A)\) = transpose of the cofactor matrix.

Properties
  • \(A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = \text{adj}(A) \cdot A = |A| \cdot I\)
  • \(|\text{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}\) (for \(n \times n\))
  • \(\text{adj}(AB) = \text{adj}(B) \cdot \text{adj}(A)\)
  • \(\text{adj}(A^T) = (\text{adj}\, A)^T\)
  • \(A^{-1} = \dfrac{\text{adj}(A)}{|A|}\)
  • \(\text{adj}(\text{adj}\, A) = |A|^{n-2} A\)

Scalar Multiplication of a Determinant

Definition

If all the elements of one row (or one column) of a determinant are multiplied by a scalar \(k\), then the value of the determinant is multiplied by \(k\).

Explanation: Each term in the expansion of the determinant contains exactly one element from that row (or column). Hence \(k\) factors out once from every term.

Scalar Multiplication of One Row

Property
\[ \begin{vmatrix} k a_1 & k a_2 & k a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} = k \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} \]

Example: Let

\[ D = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 4 - 6 = -2 \]

Multiply Row 1 by 5:

\[ D' = \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 3 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 20 - 30 = -10 = 5 \times (-2) \]
\[ \therefore \; D' = 5D \quad \checkmark \]

Scalar Multiplication of One Column

Property
\[ \begin{vmatrix} k a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ k a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ k a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} = k \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} \]

Example: Let

\[ D = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 \end{vmatrix} = 10 - 12 = -2 \]

Multiply Column 2 by 4:

\[ D' = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 12 \\ 4 & 20 \end{vmatrix} = 40 - 48 = -8 = 4 \times (-2) \]
\[ \therefore \; D' = 4D \quad \checkmark \]

Scalar Multiplication of Whole Matrix

Property

If \(A\) is a square matrix of order \(n\) and \(k\) is a scalar, then

\[ |kA| = k^n |A| \]

Explanation: Multiplying the whole matrix by \(k\) multiplies each of the \(n\) rows by \(k\). By the previous property, each row contributes one factor of \(k\), giving \(k^n\).

Example (2×2): Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad |A| = -2 \]
\[ 2A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix}, \quad |2A| = 16 - 24 = -8 \]
\[ |2A| = 2^2 \times (-2) = 4 \times (-2) = -8 \quad \checkmark \]

Example (3×3):

\[ |3A| = 3^3 |A| = 27 |A| \]

Common Mistake

Warning

\[ |kA| \neq k|A| \quad \text{(unless \(n = 1\))} \]

Wrong:

\[ \begin{vmatrix} 2a & 2b \\ 2c & 2d \end{vmatrix} \neq 2 \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} \]

Correct:

\[ \begin{vmatrix} 2a & 2b \\ 2c & 2d \end{vmatrix} = 2 \cdot 2 \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = 4 \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} \]

Splitting of a Determinant (Sum Property)

Definition

If each element of one row (or one column) of a determinant is expressed as the sum of two terms, then the determinant can be split into the sum of two determinants, with the other rows (or columns) unchanged.

Splitting Along a Row

Property
\[ \begin{vmatrix} a_1 + x_1 & a_2 + x_2 & a_3 + x_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} x_1 & x_2 & x_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} \]

Splitting Along a Column

Property
\[ \begin{vmatrix} a_1 + x_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 + x_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 + x_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} x_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ x_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ x_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} \]

Example:

\[ \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 7 \\ 2 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} 2+3 & 4+3 \\ 2 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \end{vmatrix} \]

Check:

\[ \text{LHS} = 15 - 14 = 1 \]
\[ \text{RHS} = (6 - 8) + (9 - 6) = -2 + 3 = 1 \quad \checkmark \]

Splitting Multiple Rows

Property

If two rows are sums, the determinant splits into 4 determinants. If \(n\) rows are sums, it splits into \(2^n\) determinants.

Example (two rows):

\[ \begin{vmatrix} a_1+b_1 & a_2+b_2 \\ c_1+d_1 & c_2+d_2 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ c_1 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ d_1 & d_2 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} b_1 & b_2 \\ c_1 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} b_1 & b_2 \\ d_1 & d_2 \end{vmatrix} \]

Important Warning

Warning

You cannot split like this:

\[ \begin{vmatrix} a+p & b+q \\ c+r & d+s \end{vmatrix} \neq \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} p & q \\ r & s \end{vmatrix} \]

The correct expansion (splitting Row 1 first, then Row 2) gives 4 determinants, as shown above.

Combining Splitting with Scalar Multiplication

Property
\[ \begin{vmatrix} k a_1 + b_1 & k a_2 + b_2 \\ c_1 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} = k \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ c_1 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} b_1 & b_2 \\ c_1 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} \]

Explanation: First split the row into two determinants, then pull out the scalar \(k\) from the first one. This combination is frequently used in exam problems.
